The whole picture in two minutes

Ask AI lets someone request work in ordinary language across a simulated business account. It must work out the intended outcome, find the right capabilities and records, arrange the work, ask for any necessary decisions or approval, and explain what actually happened.

The AI interprets the request.
The surrounding system makes the work happen.

Think of it as a coordinator sitting between a person and a business platform. The person describes an outcome. The coordinator turns it into a proposed workflow. The platform supplies the facts, checks its rules and performs the operations. The result comes back through the coordinator to the person.

This is a technical architecture guide. Each stage explains its inputs, outputs, algorithm, guarantees and failure modes. The end-to-end example connects those stages, and the improvement history explains why their design changed. You do not need to read the source code to follow it.

01

How the moving parts fit together

There are three overlapping flows: understanding what the person wants, doing the work, and learning whether the system did the right thing. The same component can participate in more than one, but each has a distinct job.

Follow the main path downward. Side connections supply capabilities, interpretation and account facts. Select a part to explore its role.

The conversation interface: the person’s window into the work

It accepts requests, presents one active clarification and approval previews, shows a live step checklist, and displays results. Approval previews identify affected work and blockers. Infrastructure failures can offer Retry: resending a message starts a new turn, while retrying an approval continues that turn. Reload starts a new chat. Inspection views help explain what was attempted and what changed; the interface does not decide which business operation is correct.

The conversation coordinator: continuity and progress

It brings the request, recent conversation and current progress together. It arranges planning, passes an accepted workflow on for execution, remembers a pending approval and delivers the result. If the person returns with a follow-up, it supplies recent context so that the new message can be understood in relation to the earlier one.

Capability search: the relevant part of the platform

The business platform has hundreds of possible operations. Search supplies a manageable set of relevant choices to the planner. It answers “what could be useful here?” rather than “what did the person definitely mean?”

The AI planner: interpretation and composition

It connects the person’s wording to available capabilities and proposes how they fit together. For example, a request to distribute work may need a search for leads, a search for available staff, and repeated assignments. The proposal can describe all of those relationships without the AI deciding each record change individually.

Checks and approval: the boundary between a proposal and permission

Before an operation proceeds, the system checks whether the proposal is usable and whether the real records meet the business rules. Risky or substantial changes require approval of specific effects. Neither a confident answer nor a plausible plan counts as permission.

Business operations and account records: where work becomes real state

Business operations know how to create a task, assign a lead, reply to a ticket or publish a listing. They enforce the rules for their particular business area and save the resulting changes. Account records establish facts such as who exists, what is open and where a message can be sent.

Results and explanation: close the loop honestly

The result reporter describes actual execution outcomes. It must distinguish completed work, blocked work, failed attempts and work still waiting for a decision. A proposed action and a completed action are different events.

Evaluation: observe the whole journey

Evaluation checks whether the person’s intended result was achieved, including unintended changes and misleading explanations. Those findings tell us which part of the flow needs improvement. The history shows how this feedback changed the system.

Deployment and authority boundaries

The running application is a browser client connected to a Node.js service. The service owns one local conversation and a SQLite business store. It loads the capability registry, retrieval index and local embedding model, and invokes an external planning model through a provider adapter. These are responsibilities inside the application, not a fleet of independently deployed services.

What initializes once, and what runs per request?

At startup: load registered actions and resolvers; derive action schemas and search cards; build the lexical index; load the embedding model and cached action vectors; open the business store. Per request: link names, construct search queries, embed those query texts, rank and select capabilities, render their compact contracts into the prompt, call the planner, compile and execute. On approval: resume the existing compiled plan; do not perform a fresh interpretation of the English request.

Current main offers Claude and Codex command-line adapters plus an opt-in Token Plan HTTP adapter behind the same planning interface. A requested fast or strong tier selects a configured model; it is not automatic difficulty routing. The local embedding model is separate from that generative planner. Simulated business effects and a real planning call coexist in this architecture.

02

AI foundations for a backend engineer

The application puts a probabilistic language interpreter in front of a constrained business runtime. Your existing knowledge of interfaces, type systems, query execution and transactions applies. The new work is understanding how the interpreter gets evidence, where it can be wrong, and how the surrounding system contains those errors.

A model call is an inference request

A generative language model receives a sequence of tokens and generates a continuation. Tokens are chunks of text rather than necessarily whole words. Training adjusts the model’s parameters beforehand; inference uses those parameters to produce an answer from the supplied input. Our application uses inference. A conversation or an evaluation run does not automatically update the model’s training.

The model’s learned language patterns let it connect “spread these leads across Sales” to assignment and iteration. They do not supply today’s Sales roster. That roster must come from the account. A plausible generated value can be false even when it fits an expected schema.

The context window is the working input, not a database

The context window limits how much input and generated output a model can handle in one call. The application chooses which instructions, contracts, recent exchanges and observations to send. Anything remembered by the service but omitted from the next prompt is not automatically known to that model call. More context also costs processing and can introduce competing instructions or distracting capabilities.

Prompt construction is therefore an engineering decision about the evidence available to an interpreter. Reusing a stable prompt prefix may reduce provider processing; it does not mean the provider has saved the application’s business state.

Two models, two different jobs

ModelInput → outputRole here

An embedding is a learned representation: related texts tend to have vectors pointing in similar directions. Its coordinates are not human-readable labels such as “emailness.” Normalization scales a vector to unit length; cosine similarity measures alignment, so the dot product of two normalized vectors compares their directions. Similarity is useful evidence of relevance, not a probability that an operation is correct.

Lexical search finds shared words and their variants. Semantic search can connect “hold off on webinar emails” with “pause campaign.” They fail differently: lexical search can miss paraphrases; semantic search can favor a related but wrong operation. The application combines both rankings and leaves the final intent decision to planning.

Structured output is an interface, not proof

A response schema constrains the shape of the model’s answer: response category, steps and arguments. It resembles an API contract. A valid request to the wrong API is still wrong; likewise a valid plan that invents a deadline can compile and execute successfully while failing the person’s request.

The model proposes operations as data. The application validates that data and invokes business actions itself. Tool calling does not inherently grant a model direct database access. In this app, a complete constrained plan is the normal unit of proposal.

03

The action contract connects the entire system

An action is the platform’s unit of business capability. Its contract describes a meaningful operation, the information it accepts, the result it promises, its risk and its business rules. The registry is the catalog of implemented actions and the authority used to validate and invoke them.

One example: reply to a support ticket

This is a readable contract sketch, not a wire payload. It illustrates the operation’s responsibilities without requiring an implementation reference.

Contract elementMeaningWho uses it?
Name, description and synonymsReply within the ticket conversation, rather than send an unrelated email.Retrieval finds it; the planner distinguishes it from neighboring actions.
InputThe ticket to reply to and the message to send.The planner supplies values or references; the compiler and runtime validate them.
OutputThe declared result of the ticket-reply operation.Later steps can consume declared fields; the action boundary checks the actual return value.

One action, several representations

Search card: descriptive text and relationship metadata used to rank capabilities. Planner contract: compact description plus input/output schemas used to compose a plan. Registered implementation: schemas, risk and business behavior used by compilation and execution. They are views of the same capability, with different jobs.

A producer returns a record type another action needs: searching leads supplies leads for assignment. A sibling is a related alternative that should be offered for disambiguation: ticket reply and generic email can sound similar but have different effects. A resolver maps a name and entity type to candidate records; it is not a semantic search over action descriptions.

This explains why adding a capability affects more than execution. It must become discoverable, have a comprehensible planning contract, declare usable outputs, enforce its business rules and be covered by outcome checks. The same system also needs to explain its failures honestly.

“Assign last week’s Facebook leads round-robin to available members of the Sales team, and create a follow-up task for each.”

05

Retrieval, embeddings and ranking

This is tool retrieval. It selects which business capabilities the planner sees. It does not embed the account’s contacts, tickets or leads into a vector database. Actual records are obtained through resolvers and read operations after the system knows what it needs to do.

The retrieval pipeline, in execution order

  1. Build the searchable corpus. One card per catalog action supplies its name, synonyms, business module and description, plus metadata about record types consumed and produced, related alternatives and availability. Registered actions additionally provide the input/output contracts eventually sent to the planner.
  2. Link names and carry recent context. Match capitalized name runs against current resolvers to infer possible record types and match counts. Previous-plan record types enrich the whole-message query. Previous actions can be carried into the shortlist. These hints do not resolve an ambiguous person to one record.
  3. Create several views of the request. Build a search query from the request and, for multi-part requests, each clause. Quoted text and common dictated-message phrasing are removed when useful, so a message body about a renewal does not dominate the search for a sending action. The full original request remains available to the planner. Splitting uses punctuation and conjunction heuristics. It is not a learned semantic decomposition.
  4. Run lexical and semantic retrieval. BM25 searches normalized words. The local embedding model ranks action-card vectors by similarity to the same query texts. Neither path calls the generative planner.
  5. Fuse rankings and protect clause coverage. Combine rank positions, reserve priority for the strongest lexical hits of each clause, and select a diverse initial set. A strong match for one clause should not crowd out the operation required by another.
  6. Expand for workflow completeness. Add previous actions, a bounded set of actions that amend record types made in the previous plan, lookup tools for linked entity types, related alternatives and producer operations needed to supply consumed record types. For strongly ranked actions, an unnamed related input can also need a discovery action. For “every matching contact,” an archive action needs a way to find the group even if a single contact can normally be resolved by name.
  7. Offer contracts. Send implemented selected actions’ compact contracts to the planner. Record why each was included and which capabilities were unavailable. The compiler later restricts the plan to actions actually offered during this turn.

Architectural dataflow. The application prepares semantic rankings before lexical fusion; the two branches show independent scoring responsibilities, not a promise of concurrent execution.

Lexical retrieval: BM25 over normalized action text

Both cards and queries are lowercased, tokenized, expanded for common shorthand, stripped of filler and Porter-stemmed. “Rescheduled appointments” and “reschedule appointment” converge on the same search terms. Numeric amounts and times provide little capability-selection signal and are mostly excluded from terms; their actual values remain in the request for planning.

BM25 rewards matching terms with attention to how common they are and the text’s length. The action name has weight 3, synonyms 2, module text 1.5 and description 1. Terms of at least five characters allow one edit; at least nine allow two. Multiple terms are combined permissively, so relevant partial matches survive for later ranking. A lexical score is a relevance signal, not a probability of correctness.

Embeddings: what is encoded, where it lives, and when it runs

The project uses the local, quantized bge-base-en-v1.5 embedding model. It encodes an action’s name, module, description and synonyms into a normalized vector. Query texts are encoded with the model’s retrieval instruction prefix. Similarity is the dot product of normalized vectors, equivalent to cosine similarity.

Optional depth: quantization, pooling and cache integrity

Quantization represents model weights with fewer bits, reducing resource use. Pooling reduces token-level representations to one vector for the whole text; CLS pooling uses the representation associated with a designated summary token.

Text is encoded one input at a time with normalized CLS pooling. In this quantized model, padding from batching can shift close vector scores enough to reorder candidates, so the implementation avoids that source of variation. Cached vector files are size-checked and written through a temporary file followed by rename to avoid accepting a partial write.

An embedding does not know that a customer exists, authorize a send, or choose between two identically named people. Its job here is narrower: recover a useful capability when the person’s wording shares few words with that capability’s description.

Ranking: reciprocal-rank fusion, followed by selection policy

Raw BM25 and cosine scores are on different scales. The project combines positions in ranked lists rather than adding raw scores. For a candidate action, each list contributes its weight divided by sixty plus that candidate’s one-based rank:

Fused score(action) = Σ over lists [weight ÷ (60 + rank)]

Each lexical list has weight 1, and each semantic list has default weight 1. The top 20 candidates from each semantic list join fusion. With both paths active, an action ranked first in both receives 1/61 + 1/61 ≈ 0.03279; an action ranked first in only one receives ≈ 0.01639. These values are ranking scores, not confidence percentages.

What happens if retrieval misses?

The planner can request up to two additional capability searches. Already offered actions are excluded and new contracts are appended. The second search includes guidance that matching and excluding lists are workflow operations, not necessarily missing business actions. An unsupported response can trigger the first unused search; the system replans if it adds contracts. Retrieval recovery does not guarantee completion.

Why these defaults?

The architectural rationale is documented: use a small relevant shortlist to reduce prompt size; protect clauses to preserve compound requests; cap modules to prevent one domain dominating; add producers to make workflows executable; combine local embeddings with lexical retrieval to recover paraphrases without another remote API call.

The exact values—fifteen initial slots, six per module, fusion constant sixty and the listed thresholds—are current configuration. The evidence presented here does not establish each as an independently optimized value. A change should be evaluated for required-capability recall, final shortlist size, end-to-end correctness and latency on held-out requests. A larger shortlist can improve recall while making the planner’s choice harder.

Metadata makes retrieval executable

Module-owned synonyms augment generated card wording for lexical and embedding search. Discovery helpers must find existing records; an action that creates a record is not automatically a suitable lookup producer. Optional relationships expand only for relevant typed mentions, reducing irrelevant contracts. The initial per-module cap has an exception: a candidate with nonempty entity metadata bypasses that cap when every consumed and produced entity type is mentioned. This does not bypass the other relevance rules or guarantee selection.

Planner input and workflow contract

The generative model is used for interpretation and workflow composition. It receives a deliberately limited view of the platform and returns a constrained response. Retrieval, name resolution, validation, transactions and execution remain application responsibilities.

The workflow language is deliberately small

A plan can invoke an action, resolve a name to a record, filter a list, exclude a set of identifiers, or iterate a flat list of action calls. Iteration can rotate through a second list for round-robin assignment. References pass earlier outputs to later steps, select fields from lists, and interpolate values into text.

This is a dataflow graph: dependencies come from references. The current executor runs independent name resolutions first, then the remaining ordered steps; the graph does not imply parallel scheduling. Iteration is flat, with no nested arbitrary programs, general arithmetic or unrestricted scripting. Passing an owner identifier unchanged can stay in the graph. Deriving a new deadline from a stored date can require a look and another planner decision.

Tokens, variability and provider adapters

The model processes the instructions, contracts, context and generated response as tokens. Retrieving a smaller useful contract set reduces input compared with supplying the full catalog. Observation and repair add calls and repeated input. Provider adapters normalize the structured reply, model name, latency and reported usage; cached input, visible output and reasoning usage are distinct when the provider reports them.

Stable instructions and response schemas make prefix reuse possible, but provider caching is not guaranteed. Sampling settings do not prove deterministic interpretation. The model still can produce a valid but semantically wrong workflow, so correctness is assessed through actual execution outcomes and explicit expectations.

Keep the requested destination and effect

Fresh planner replies declare the intended effect, final catalog operations and optional delivery destination. The coordinator carries the declaration through search and repair, but it can release a rejected read outcome and restate change actions after a look. For a request that asks a question and then requests a change, a read outcome that describes only the observation can be replaced by the subsequent change outcome. Outcome checking is a consistency check on the model’s declaration, not an independent intent verifier. This is a model-facing selection check, not user approval and not proof that the condition expresses the request correctly. Iterations and round-robin inputs may be literal lists of identities or references. A later call within an item can use the immediately preceding call’s output, allowing creation followed by a dependent operation inside the same transaction. Filters support null-inclusive conditions. Iteration remains flat: two stages of fan-out require separate list-producing steps and loops, not arbitrary nested programs.

A usable plan can still be the wrong plan

Suppose the person asks for a task and the proposal adds a deadline that was never requested. The deadline may be perfectly valid. The task may be created successfully. The result still fails to respect the original request.

“Multi-turn” can refer to several different mechanisms. They have different state, different costs and different retry semantics. Treating them as one generic agent loop hides the architecture.

The bounded planning state machine

The initial call can return any of the six response categories. Budgets apply to particular transitions, not a single simple call count for the entire interaction. Up to two capability searches and 2 read-only observation rounds are available. A second observation can depend on the first. A read-outcome request that asks to observe again can instead finish through the existing read plan. The allowance table explains the remaining branches.

  1. Search branch. Search the planner’s missing-capability query, exclude contracts already seen, append new ones, rebuild the planning brief and call again. A third search request terminates as unsupported.
  2. Look branch. Compile the proposed observation, ensure all invoked actions have read risk, run it as the same actor and return per-step outputs or errors. Observation text is capped at 16000 characters. A subsequent plan can reuse the read steps as dependencies.
  3. Repair branch. Supply the rejected proposal and explicit compiler errors. One new call may correct it. A second plan repair is allowed only if the first plan repair reduced the number of errors. Other repair paths have their own limits; a parsed but invalid response envelope uses the shared repair allowance.
  4. Terminal branch. A valid plan proceeds to execution; a question, answer, unsupported decision or planning failure returns to the person.

Each planner call has a default deadline of 120 seconds. The turn as a whole has a budget too: at most 8 planner calls and 180 seconds. Every continuation in this chapter—search, observation, repair and resampling—spends from that one budget, and each call’s deadline shrinks to the time the turn has left. The individual allowances keep any one kind of continuation from repeating; the budget bounds their combination, so one request cannot become an open-ended conversation with the model. A timeout aborts the provider call and is categorized as infrastructure failure. A turn that runs out of budget stops before execution, so nothing has changed.

The person can also end a turn. If the browser stops listening—a closed tab or a reload—the call in flight is aborted, and a plan that was already made does not run. An approved run is different: approval is a decision the person has already made, and leaving the page does not withdraw it.

Each model call also leaves a record: its position in the turn, the model, fingerprints of the stable instructions and of the planning brief, the time spent waiting and the time the provider reports, attempts, whether it was a repair, and reported usage. The record answers questions a final outcome cannot: which call in the turn went wrong, whether two calls saw identical instructions, and where the time went. It travels with the streamed events and is saved with each evaluation turn. It is evidence about the planning process, not about whether the plan was right.

Observe only when interpretation requires it

“Assign the task to the lead’s owner” can pass the owner identity from a read directly to the write. No extra model reasoning is needed. “Move its due date one week later” needs the stored date before a new value can be computed. A look provides that fact to the planner. If the operation itself supports “new day, keep existing local time,” the business action can preserve the unchanged time without another AI calculation.

The observation budget prevents repeated speculative reads from turning into an unbounded loop. Failed observation steps are returned as evidence; observation is not permission to write. Action descriptions that preserve unchanged business details reduce both planning ambiguity and extra rounds.

The comparison agent uses a different loop

The project also contains a step-by-step baseline: choose one action, observe the result, then choose the next. It provides an experimental comparison with whole-plan composition. Whole-plan execution can process many deterministic handoffs and items after one planning decision, while the step-by-step approach returns to the model between choices. How permission and business rules shape the journey

The AI’s proposal is not an authority to act. The surrounding platform owns whether an operation is permitted, whether a record is eligible and whether the person must approve its effects.

Approval is tied to known changes, such as particular recipients, messages or records. It is not a general instruction to do whatever later seems useful. A small internal change may proceed directly; external, destructive and sufficiently large grouped changes require approval under the current policy.

Approve the eligible part, with the blocked part visible

Suppose the person asks for replies to two tickets. One has a usable recipient; the other has no email address. The system should preserve the original scope, explain the blocker and ask whether to proceed with the eligible reply. It should not quietly discard the blocked ticket or invent an address.

After approval, it performs the eligible work and reports the blocked work separately. Some simple batches support this preview; more dependent workflows cannot always predict every later eligibility condition before earlier changes happen.

Rules belong to the business operation

A ticket reply is more than an email. It also belongs to a ticket conversation and may affect its response history. Publishing a listing is more than editing a phone number. Keeping those effects together prevents a superficially similar operation from bypassing the intended business meaning.

Untrusted text is not permission

A note inside an account record might contain instructions that conflict with the person’s request. It should remain information to interpret, not gain authority simply because the model read it. Instructions help guide behavior; the platform’s independent boundaries still matter.

This remains a local simulator with limited identity and permission controls, rather than a complete production access system. The architectural responsibility is clear even where the current implementation is incomplete.

Approval is a binding over proposed effects

The executor assigns risk from the registered action, not from the planner’s wording. Destructive and external actions require confirmation. A repeated-write step over 3 or more eligible items also requires confirmation. This is a loop-level bulk rule, not a universal sum of every write anywhere in a request.

An approval binds the relevant step, the compiled plan fingerprint and a fingerprint of the concrete write targets and arguments. Where preflight supplies business bindings—such as recipient details—those are included too, along with known blocked items. The preview is limited to ten write-call entries and ten blocker entries with counts; a preview sample is not the complete internal target set used for binding. When several independent sends follow one another and each can be checked in advance, they share a single approval. The preview then lists every step that approval decides, so the person sees everything that will be sent, not only the first message.

A write must see everything its search matched

Searches return a page of results: a default number of records unless a limit is given. That is fine for reading—“show me some recent leads.” It is dangerous for writing. “Tag every lead from the expo” must tag every matching lead, not the first page of them. A workflow that silently acted on one page would report success while leaving work undone.

When a search feeds a write, the executor therefore asks for every match, up to a fixed maximum of 500. If the search matched more than it loaded, the write is refused and nothing changes; the planner learns how many records matched and is asked to narrow the search and work in parts. The same reasoning applies to a filter or exclusion: if the list it checks against was cut short, records it should have kept or removed are missing, so the write that depends on it is refused too.

A limit the person actually asked for is different. “The ten newest leads” means ten. The difficulty is telling a requested limit from one the model supplied: planners tend to copy the schema’s default of 50, or choose a generous number “to be safe.” The application decides from what the person said in the turns the planner saw, not from the plan. A limit of 50 or more that appears nowhere in the person’s words is ignored. Numbers that measure something else—“$50”, “50%”, “50 days”—do not count as asking for 50 records. Small limits implied by wording such as “the newest” or “top three” are kept.

This is a rule about pagination and stated intent, not proof that the search itself was right. A complete search for the wrong condition still needs evaluation.

On resume, reads run again and targets are prepared again. If a fingerprint differs, the old approval is insufficient and the system asks again. Multiple independent gates retain their earlier approvals, so answering a later gate does not lose permission already granted to an earlier one. The binding does not turn the entire conversation into a transaction or freeze all facts used during reasoning.

Preflight and action execution are different operations

A preflight checks supported business eligibility without invoking the handler, creating records, consuming injected faults or emitting delivery traces. For supported independent sends, it separates eligible and blocked work before approval. It cannot generally predict a multi-call item whose earlier actions create prerequisites for later calls. The action boundary still enforces rules at execution time.

Dynamic text is escaped when inserted into the planning brief so it cannot close application-defined delimiters. This is useful structural hygiene, not a complete prompt-injection defense. The important enforcement boundaries remain the offered-action restriction, actor/business checks, risk policy and concrete approval binding. The local simulator’s identity controls do not amount to production multi-tenant authorization.

Execution, replay and error handling

Execution turns a compiled dependency plan into concrete calls and committed state. The important guarantees are scoped: validation applies at each call, transactions group one item’s work, and replay protection applies to the same execution identity.

Execution semantics: transactions, dependencies and replay

The executor first runs independent name-resolution steps, then walks the remaining compiled steps in order, stores their outputs and substitutes references as needed. A fully failed or skipped dependency causes its dependent steps to be skipped; independent steps can still run. A partially completed iteration can expose its result structure, including empty results for failed items, so partial data must not be mistaken for universal success.

Each iteration item runs its flat group of calls inside one SQLite transaction. If any call or its output validation fails, that item’s changes, audit records and recorded writes roll back together. Other items have independent transactions. There is no turn-wide atomic transaction, and cancellation at a later gate leaves earlier committed independent changes intact.

For the six-lead example, one item might assign a lead and create its task. A task-creation failure rolls back that item’s assignment too. Five other items can remain committed. The displayed attempted assignment trace may still exist even though its business change rolled back.

Idempotency has a scope

Successful writes are recorded under a key composed of execution identity, step identity, item identity and call position. Resuming that same execution reuses recorded outputs for those writes instead of invoking them again; reads rerun. Record identifiers are preferred for item identity, with position as a fallback. Stable item identities matter if a list’s order changes between runs.

A new English request receives a new execution identity, even if its wording is equivalent. This is scoped replay protection, not universal duplicate-intent detection or a guarantee of exactly-once effects in an external service. All current delivery effects are simulated inside the local platform. Real remote effects would need their own failure and deduplication design beyond the local transaction.

Observability: inspect the transition that failed

The service streams server-sent events containing retrieval decisions, planner requests and replies, compiler errors, action calls, execution transitions, run outcomes, state differences and audit additions. A final transport event closes the request. The local server serializes ask/confirm activity over its conversation and rejects overlapping requests as busy.

Use the layers in order: Was the needed capability offered? Inspect retrieval rank and inclusion reasons. Did the planner compose it correctly? Inspect the structured proposal and compile feedback. Could it execute? Inspect arguments, eligibility, approval and per-item errors. What actually remained? Inspect the final run, committed state differences and audit. A call trace records an attempt and can outlive transaction rollback.

The normal execution explanation is constructed from run results, without a second generative summarizer. Reads used only as inputs stay quiet; terminal reads provide an answer. Bounded summaries can omit detail, so this reduces invented-success risk without replacing state-based evaluation.

Resolve early and recover only under explicit conditions

Independent name resolutions run before other work. Dependent scoped resolutions stay in dependency order. Once a resolution reports a missing or unusable name, subsequent write steps are skipped. This protects against some partially executed requests; it does not make the whole request atomic or undo earlier work before a later dependent lookup fails.

The conversation can start at most one replacement plan when the first run qualifies for recovery and no new audit entries were recorded. One general branch accepts fully failed runs whose errors are invalid arguments, not-found errors or certain prerequisite conflicts. A separate lookup-miss branch requires affirmative evidence that no write was attempted, even if an attempted write would have rolled back. It can recover an identifiable lookup miss in a failed or partial run, or a completed write loop that processed no items because an upstream search was empty. Ordinary empty reads remain valid results; filtering a nonempty search down to nothing does not alone meet that empty-search condition. The retry receives the empty search and other useful findings, and an unchanged repeat search is sent back for repair. The replacement is a turn in its own right, with its own identity and a new execution identity; if it pauses for approval, that approval answers the replacement, not the plan that failed. Approval resumes use the saved execution instead and never enter this retry. Similarly, when an observation precedes the final plan, the planner is told the names of the observation’s steps, so the final plan can refer back to exactly what was read.

Runtime convenience is bounded by the action contract

If a standalone create fails with a duplicate that identifies an existing record of the same entity type, and a later step depends on that create, the executor can supply that existing record to the dependent step. A create with no dependent step still reports the duplicate. Optional arguments supplied by a reference that resolves to no value can be omitted when the input is optional and does not accept null. These rules do not turn every duplicate or missing value into success.

Failure does not always mean the same rollback boundary

A transactional item still rolls back when a call throws. Some owned communication actions instead record an unsuccessful delivery as a business result so that a ticket, review request or listing retains its attempt trail. Distinguish a thrown transaction failure, a committed failed-delivery record and overall partial completion. Report what actually persisted rather than inferring it from the word “failed.”

Verdict, record cards and execution evidence

For a record question, the coordinator can run reads as an observation and ask the model for a short yes/no/count verdict. The final read plan runs again. The verdict is included only when execution finishes successfully; deterministic summaries and available record cards can also describe other outcomes. Write summaries remain derived from outcomes. A verdict based on earlier observations is not a guarantee of snapshot consistency if facts change between reads. Structured record cards expose readable fields and named parts without requiring the user to interpret raw identifiers.

Approval resume also reruns reads. This helps current eligibility and target checks, but is not a general snapshot-isolation guarantee covering the entire conversation.

A deadline calculated during planning can become stale even if the referenced task identity remains unchanged. Approval fingerprints bind the concrete proposed effects, not every premise from which a model derived them. The fixed business clock makes relative dates repeatable in this simulator; it is separate from the wall clock used to measure request latency.

Follow-up continuity has a precise boundary

“Make those tasks high priority” gets recent language context, relevant prior actions and a bounded working set of up to 12 observed typed identities, including successful loop writes. Retrieval may offer actions to modify types made in the previous turn. A remake check can nudge a proposal toward changing existing records, but it is not a guarantee. The system may search again or clarify; identities are neither fresh record contents nor a complete durable selection. Business changes can survive a restart while conversation state and pending approvals do not. The browser starts a new chat on reload.

Working identities and the business clock

The working record set stores entity, identity and display name, sometimes with a related lead or contact identity, rather than complete mutable records. It includes completed resolution and top-level action results as well as successful write results from completed or partial loops; loop reads are not retained there. Up to twelve identities supplement the last four dialogue pairs and action carry-over. Lookup results still need current eligibility checks. The cap, incomplete observations and process lifetime prevent this from being an exact durable selection.

How we know whether the whole flow works

A convincing final sentence is weak evidence. A good evaluation checks the requested outcome against the actual business state, including changes that should not have happened.

For “create a task for Maya,” that means the right task exists, belongs to the right person, has only the requested details and did not alter unrelated records. For a declined batch, the important result is that the declined changes did not happen.

Check the platform’s rules

Known workflows can be exercised directly to check permissions, grouped failure, approval and repeat protection. These checks establish the behavior of the surrounding system.

Evaluate interpretation end to end

Give a real planner ordinary-language requests over repeatable fictional data, then inspect the final outcomes. Repeated trials reveal whether the same request is handled consistently.

Finding every required capability, producing an acceptable proposal and achieving the right business outcome are separate measures. An improvement in one does not prove an improvement in the others. A small set of repeated successes also does not establish reliability across every operation.

Compare like with like

Changed questions, changed starting data or a different model can change the score. If test expectations were wrong, correcting them is legitimate, but the new score is no longer a clean comparison with the old test. Provider outages should be reported separately, with any resulting gaps in coverage made visible.

Speed and cost follow the flow too

Every extra planning round can add processing, waiting and usage. A workflow that passes routine results along without another AI decision can be quicker. Gathering necessary facts or correcting a mistake may justify an extra round.

The useful goal is time and usage per correctly completed task. A cheap wrong answer is not a successful optimization. An approval preview can be a useful first response even though the work is not yet complete; waiting for the person’s approval is a different kind of delay from waiting for the model.

Subscription allowance and estimated API prices are different accounting measures. The current project does not turn every provider’s usage into an authoritative dollar cost.

How the end-to-end flow improved

The project evolved by observing where complete requests broke down. Sometimes the fix improved what the AI could see. Sometimes it simplified a business operation. Sometimes the problem was the way results were explained or measured.

The repeated pattern was observe a failure → identify the responsible part → make a focused change → check the whole journey → keep, revise or discard the change.

7 improvement stories. Open one to follow the reasoning.

1. Find the right capabilities before asking for a better answer

What went wrong?

Requests used wording that did not match operation names, and a person’s name did not reveal which kind of record it referred to.

What changed?

Richer descriptions and related vocabulary improved word search. Name hints helped distinguish people and record types. Meaning-based search then helped recover paraphrases.

What did we learn from the result?

Saved retrieval experiments found more of the required capabilities. That improved the information available to planning, while leaving the final decision about intent to the planner.

The wider lessonBetter interpretation begins with better choices and facts. A capability that was never offered cannot be selected reliably.

Technical mechanism

This changes the information-selection boundary: lexical vocabulary, entity hints and semantic rank fusion affect which contracts reach planning. Measure required-tool recall separately from the correctness of the eventual workflow.

2. Tell the truth about a blocked record

What went wrong?

A request to assign work to a deactivated person was answered as though the person did not exist.

What changed?

The lookup process retained enough information to distinguish an existing but unusable person from a genuinely missing one.

What did we learn from the result?

The targeted assignment failure was reported correctly in the saved follow-up trials. Other unrelated weaknesses remained.

The wider lessonAn eligibility problem needs a truthful business explanation. It is not necessarily a language-understanding problem.

Technical mechanism

This changes resolution and failure classification. An existing inactive record yields a conflict rather than a missing-record result. It is a platform correction, without needing another planner capability.

3. Simplify the request the system has to solve

What went wrong?

“Move the meeting to Monday, same time” sometimes led to unnecessary questions or a workflow that found the meeting but never moved it.

What changed?

An extra fact-gathering step was useful in some situations. But a later shortcut treated an incomplete proposal as though it were that step, used up its opportunity to read useful facts and made results worse. That shortcut was discarded. The retained approach let the person specify the new day while the platform preserved the existing local time.

What did we learn from the result?

The saved scheduling trials became more consistent after the simpler business operation and better handling of incomplete proposals. The failed experiment remains part of the history.

The wider lessonDo not force the AI to reconstruct an unchanged detail when the business operation can preserve it. A plausible shortcut still needs measurement.

Technical mechanism

This changes both the observation loop and the action contract. The accepted design preserves an unchanged time inside the reschedule operation; compilation also rejects plans that only resolve records and invoke no action. These address different failure mechanisms.

4. Keep each business action’s meaning intact

What went wrong?

Sending an email could look similar to replying to a ticket, but only the latter should update the ticket’s own response history. New business areas also introduced new forms of ambiguity and related work.

What changed?

Each operation kept responsibility for its complete business effect. Closely related capabilities were described more clearly, and new areas were checked across search, identity, saved state and user-visible outcomes.

What did we learn from the result?

The platform grew into a broader set of meaningful operations rather than a catalog of unconditional success responses. Broader coverage also created more competing choices for planning.

The wider lessonA new capability affects the whole flow. It changes what can be found, chosen, approved, performed and meaningfully tested.

Technical mechanism

This changes the capability contracts and the business-action boundary. Similar-looking operations need distinct descriptions and complete domain effects, then input/output validation and business-state assertions.

5. Remember the supporting work hidden inside a request

What went wrong?

“Archive every matching contact” could surface the archive action without a useful way to find the whole group. Other workflows performed reads but gave the person an empty explanation.

What changed?

Bulk requests were given better search support, and results from repeated reads became more useful in the final explanation. Some business operations also became easier to address without asking people for internal record identifiers.

What did we learn from the result?

Later wider-platform trials showed stronger results. The test cases changed during that sequence, so the score increases cannot all be credited to one isolated fix.

The wider lessonThe requested change often depends on finding, selecting and explaining records. Supporting steps are part of the product experience.

Technical mechanism

This changes producer expansion for bulk requests and terminal-read reporting. A write needs a source list; a loop of reads needs a visible answer. Improved execution without improved reporting would still leave the user unable to assess the result.

6. Let the person decide whether partial work is acceptable

What went wrong?

Two requested ticket replies could include one eligible ticket and one without a usable recipient. Silently dropping the blocked ticket changed the person’s scope; treating eligibility as a missing intention asked the wrong question.

What changed?

The system checked which work was possible, disclosed the blocked part and requested approval for the known eligible part.

What did we learn from the result?

Focused saved evaluations exercised the policy, and the recorded mixed outcome above shows one approved reply alongside one clearly reported blocker. More dependent workflows still have limits on what can be previewed in advance.

The wider lessonSeparate intended scope, factual eligibility and authorization. The person should decide whether the useful subset is worth proceeding with.

Technical mechanism

This changes preflight and approval binding. Eligible effects and known blockers are prepared separately, disclosed together and included in the approval fingerprint. Eligibility does not silently redefine the requested scope.

7. Improve the measurement along with the assistant

What went wrong?

Provider allowance failures looked like an accuracy regression. Some expectations became wrong as the platform gained capabilities, and adding new account data changed experimental conditions.

What changed?

Operational failures were separated from completed evaluations. Changed expectations were identified, different groups of scenarios were kept distinct, and repeatable starting conditions were tracked.

What did we learn from the result?

The resulting reports became more honest about what was measured, what was untested and what could be compared. Remaining failures were preserved rather than explained away.

The wider lessonA trustworthy improvement story includes failed ideas, changed tests and uncertainty. It is not a single upward score.

Technical mechanism

This changes the evaluation boundary: identify operational failures, preserve independent scenario groups and check state rather than a success label. A changed test expectation also changes what a score comparison can establish.

Check your understanding of the flows

Try explaining each situation before opening the answer. The goal is to locate the responsibility, not memorize terminology.

1. A perfectly valid task has an unwanted deadline

The person asked for a task but never mentioned a date. The system created it successfully with a deadline. Where did the flow go wrong?

Think it through

Execution may have worked correctly, but interpretation added an unrequested detail. Validity checks do not prove faithful intent. A stronger evaluation would check that no deadline was added, and clearer planning guidance should discourage the invention.

2. The assistant asks you to list everyone on Sales

The platform can read team membership, but the planner did not receive a useful way to do so. Should you immediately upgrade the model?

Think it through

First examine the information boundary. A readable account fact should normally be gathered by the system. Better capability selection or another search may fix the missing support. Asking the person to reproduce account data is different from asking for a decision only that person can make.

3. One approved ticket reply succeeds and another cannot be sent

What must the person be told, and what should the final outcome establish?

Think it through

The person should know the original scope, the blocked ticket and reason, and the exact eligible work they are approving. The result should explain both outcomes. Checking only that the reply sounds successful would miss an unsent ticket, an unauthorized send or an inaccurate claim about completion.

A useful way to explain the architecture to someone else is to take one request and answer five questions: What is understood? What facts are needed? Who decides? What changes? How do we know?

4. A successful run changed no records

A search found no company, and a following write loop ran over an empty list. Another read found contacts with the requested company name in their own fields. What would you inspect before reporting success?

Think it through

Check whether any write was attempted, what the empty search actually queried and what the other read found. The empty-search recovery can try one replacement plan with that evidence, rather than assume the business request had no matches. A second plan that repeats the same empty search unchanged can be returned for repair. If a search found records and a correct filter excluded all of them, an empty outcome may be appropriate.

Technical checkpoints

A needed capability ranks first semantically but has no lexical overlap. Can it survive?

It is among the semantic top ten and is therefore exempt from the lexical score floor. That alone does not guarantee inclusion: clause-priority slots, the module cap and the low-match heuristic also affect initial selection. In particular, a very low whole-query lexical score with no name hint can clear initial ranked picks. Semantic relevance and final offered membership are different stages.

A proposal connects a list of contacts to an argument requiring one contact. Where is that caught?

The compiler rejects incompatible list-to-scalar bindings, including the staff-list binding inside our worked iteration. A supported top-level projection of scalar values can instead be checked at runtime: exactly one is unwrapped, zero fails, and several require clarification. Compatible shape and a valid identity still do not prove the intended person was selected.

A task was created, then a later approval gate paused the request. Will approving create that task again?

Resuming the same execution reuses its successful recorded write instead of invoking it again. Reads rerun, and new targets may require approval again. Sending a new equivalent English request has a new execution identity and does not receive this deduplication automatically.

Today

What this version provides

The current platform has 500 executable operations across 50 business areas. Those areas span customer relationships, communication, scheduling, commerce, content, learning, reporting and administration.

The business account and external effects are simulated. A configured planner uses a real model; capability search also uses a local meaning model. The project therefore exercises real interpretation over a controlled business environment.

It supports direct requests, multi-step workflows, recent conversational context, focused questions, approval of concrete changes, partial outcomes and inspection of results. It also includes a comparison agent and evaluation tools for studying quality, time and usage.

Important limits remain: conversation continuity is not durable recovery, previous result sets are not preserved as exact permanent selections, some planning requests still fail, and the local permission model is not a complete production identity system. These limits help explain the current boundaries; they do not change the intended responsibility of each part.
